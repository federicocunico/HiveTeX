Graph Networks for Protein Folding

F. Cunico · A. Lindqvist · K. Tanaka
Abstract. We predict tertiary structure from contact maps using message passing over residue graphs.
1 Introduction

Folding is ΔG-driven [1]. Most residues settle into a small set of local motifs, which a graph model can learn from contact maps alone.

We describe the model in Section 2 and the training data in Section 3.

Built for papers with more than one author

Everything Overleaf users expect, without handing your unpublished work to a cloud.

Live co-writing

See each co-author's cursor in their own color, follow them through the document, and never merge by hand.

Works offline

Keep writing on a train. When you reconnect, changes merge paragraph by paragraph.

Compiled by the hive

The fastest computer online builds the PDF and sends it to everyone. If it leaves, the next one takes over.

Private by design

Sources travel encrypted between co-authors' devices. The server only knows who is in the project.

GitHub backup

Commit to your own repository after a burst of edits, or when you stop typing for a while.

Bring your project

Import an Overleaf .zip or a folder, or keep the project mirrored to a folder your other tools can read.

From blank page to shared PDF

Three steps, and the second one takes about ten seconds.

  1. STEP 1

    Start from a template

    Article, thesis, Beamer slides or a CV. Each one compiles before you change a word.

  2. STEP 2

    Share the project ID

    Send gnn-folding-2026 and its password. You choose who can edit and who can only read.

  3. STEP 3

    Write together

    Edits appear as they are typed. The PDF refreshes for everyone after each compile.

Graph Networks for Protein Folding from Contact Maps

Federico Cunico · Ada Lindqvist · Kenji Tanaka
University of Verona · KTH Stockholm · RIKEN
AbstractWe predict tertiary structure from predicted contact maps using message passing over residue graphs. On CASP15 targets the model reaches a median GDT-TS of 71.4 while training on a single GPU.

1  Introduction

Proteins fold into the conformation that minimises free energy [1]. Predicting that state from sequence alone remains hard, because the search space grows as 3N for N residues.

Recent work treats contacts as a graph [?], and we follow that view with a message-passing network over residue pairs:

hi(t+1) = φ( hi(t), Σj ψ(hj(t), eij) )(1)

Section 2 describes the model, and Section 3 reports results on CASP15.

Newcomer reading

  • H1
    Status in sentences. "Outdated: 2 edits since last compile", "All changes synced", "Compiled on kenji-workstation (fastest online)". No unexplained pills or leader jargon.
  • H9
    Errors that teach. The Problems panel turns Undefined control sequence into the cause and a one-click fix. The raw log is one click away.
  • H6
    The toolbar inserts LaTeX for you. Section, bold, lists, math, symbols, figures, tables, citations and references, each with a tooltip that shows the LaTeX it writes.
  • H10
    A tour, once. Four steps (editor, rail, compile, share), skippable, and it can be reopened from Help.

Experienced reading

  • H7
    Palette for everything. Ctrl K runs actions, opens files, jumps to outline entries (@) or lines (:), and shows each shortcut so it can be learned.
  • H3
    Chrome you control. Hide the formatting toolbar, collapse the side panel, drag or keyboard-resize the split, or press Ctrl Shift F for focus mode. The layout is remembered per device.
  • COLLAB
    Presence you can act on. Each person has one color for avatar, cursor, selection and file dot. Click an avatar to follow them.
  • H4
    Familiar conventions. Overleaf's source/PDF split, a VS Code-style rail and palette, and SyncTeX double-click both ways.
